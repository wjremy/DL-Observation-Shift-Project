## Our claims

- C1: the raw drop from A to B confounds fit on A with robustness; judge models against the drop a Bayes-optimal model of A would also have.
- C2: the trade-off is lopsided: a small discount of the testing evidence costs second order at A and gains first order at B; partial trust beats both ends.
- C3: a deployment-time dial weighting the testing-process evidence in a latent-state model, set per hospital.
- C4: recalibration and thresholds cannot repair observation shift because they keep the ranking; the dial does.
- C5: how much local data the dial needs, compared with recalibration and full fine-tuning.
- C6: re-learning only the testing component on unlabelled data from the new hospital.

## Verdict per claim

| Claim | Status | Closest work and what it leaves open |
|---|---|---|
| C1 | partly known | Taori et al. 2020 (effective robustness) and Miller et al. 2021 (accuracy on the line) already warn that out-of-distribution scores confound source accuracy, and adjust empirically with a trend across models. Nobody we found uses the Bayes-optimal source model's own drop as the justified cost, and no clinical benchmark applies this to a changed testing policy. |
| C2 | mechanism known, application open | The local asymmetry follows from risk geometry (anchor regression, Rothenhäusler et al. 2021; semi-modular inference, Carmona & Nicholls 2020; Frazier & Nott 2026). For missingness, **Rockenschaub et al. 2024** show that ignorable shifts leave the Bayes predictor unchanged (our E3), and that under non-ignorable shifts ignoring missingness helped most when it was highly informative. **Futoma et al. 2021** found that fitting with measurement indicators and dropping them at deployment transferred better across hospitals. Both compare all or nothing; partial trust, and why it beats both ends, is not shown. Condition to state: the gain at B is first order only if discounting moves predictions toward B's optimum (true for a shift toward less selective testing, not for every shift). |
| C3 | ingredients known, combination apparently open | Tempering one component's influence is established (power likelihoods; cut models, Plummer 2015; semi-modular inference with a tunable influence parameter chosen by predictive score, Carmona & Nicholls 2020). Models with an explicit testing or observation process exist (Alaa et al. 2017; Lange et al. 2015; Rubanova et al. 2019; DeepJoint, Jeanselme et al. 2022, which is more robust to a weekday/weekend change in clinical presence). None weights the testing evidence with a per-hospital dial. Wording to avoid: "neural networks cannot have such a lever"; correct: "standard architectures do not expose one". |
| C4 | known as such | Recalibration and thresholds preserve ranking and AUC (Janssen et al. 2008; Yang, Soltan & Clifton 2022 across four NHS trusts; Guo et al. 2017). Our contribution is not this fact but showing that the dial restores ranking where they cannot (E5: AUC 0.64 to 0.75 at B). |
| C5 | framework known, comparison open | The updating ladder and its sample-size logic are established (Steyerberg et al. 2004; Vergouwe et al. 2017; Riley et al. 2020); silent trials exist (Kwong et al. 2022). No study measures the data needed for an observation-evidence weight against recalibration and fine-tuning. |
| C6 | partly known | Adaptation to missingness shift with unlabelled target data exists: Zhou, Balakrishnan & Lipton 2023 (linear models, specific missingness assumptions) and Stokes et al. 2025 (preprint; MNAR, imputation and weighting, two-hospital EHR). Neither refits only an observation-process component while holding physiology fixed. |

## Gap statement (draft for the proposal)

Clinical prediction models exploit informative testing patterns, and these patterns change between hospitals. Prior work either evaluates such shifts with raw performance drops, which reward models that under-fit the source, or chooses between using and ignoring missingness altogether (Futoma et al. 2021; Rockenschaub et al. 2024). Methods that adapt to missingness shift adjust the whole predictor (Zhou et al. 2023; Stokes et al. 2025), and per-site recalibration cannot repair it because it keeps the ranking of patients. Separately, Bayesian statistics offers ways to temper the influence of one model component (Carmona & Nicholls 2020), and clinical latent-state models can represent the testing process explicitly (Alaa et al. 2017). We bring these together: a per-hospital weight on the evidence from the testing process, motivated by the lopsided trade-off between source and target error, evaluated against the drop that a Bayes-optimal source model would also have, and set with the limited local data of a silent run-in.

## After reading the closest papers in full (Claude, 19 Oct 2026)

**Futoma, Simons, Doshi-Velez & Kamaleswaran 2021, Critical Care Explorations (PMC8238368).** Logistic regressions for vasopressor onset at two hospitals: Beth Israel (MIMIC-III; the same hospital as PhysioNet 2019 set A) and Methodist LeBonheur, Memphis. Physiology features versus binary measurement indicators. Indicator-only models transfer poorly. Combined models are best in-sample but not across sites. In a post hoc analysis, the combined model's coefficients used *without* the indicator terms at deployment generalised best for Beth Israel-derived models, beating both the combined and the physiology-only models; for Methodist-derived models the combined model usually stayed best. Their reading: fitting with the indicators adjusts the physiology coefficients for practice, so "fit with, deploy without". This is the dial at w = 0 in a linear model; they tried only 0 and 1. Two consequences: (1) intermediate w is the open, natural next step; (2) a linear dial (scale the indicator coefficients by w) is a cheap, strong baseline we can run on the PhysioNet data, whose hospital A is their Beth Israel.

**Rockenschaub et al. 2024, arXiv 2406.16484 (tabular, non-temporal).** Theorem: under ignorable (MAR) missingness in both environments, the Bayes predictor is unchanged by a missingness shift (first shown by Zhou et al. 2023); under MNAR it can change. Experiments with simulated tabular data and LBIDD covariates, shifts of the missingness rate (50% to 25% or 0%), evaluated against the analytical Bayes predictor. Learned estimators were not reliably robust even under ignorable shifts; ignoring informative missingness (MICE) was most beneficial when missingness was highly informative, worse in the source but more robust. Consequences: our E3 theory is known, and E3's empirical result (networks lose under a shift where the Bayes rule is unchanged) agrees with theirs, now for sequence models. C1 is closer to known than the agent report said: they already use the analytical Bayes predictor as the reference under missingness shift, though not as the yardstick for ranking architectures, and not for time series. They compare all-or-nothing strategies, not partial trust.

**CLIO, "Policy-Aware Pretraining for EHR as Clinician-in-the-Loop Dynamical Systems" (Fu, Hu, Tao et al., OpenReview submission; read from the text Wesley supplied).** A foundation model that separates physiology (controlled SDE latent) from the observation and action policy (marked point processes), with an invariance penalty across hospitals, inverse-propensity reweighting, counterfactual contrastive training, preference alignment and federated training. The environment affects only the policy channels; policy parameters are site-specific with a meta-learned initialisation for adaptation. Overlap: the separation of physiology from testing policy (our E5 structure) and site-specific policy parameters (close to C6). Not covered: any weight on how much the testing evidence counts, the trade-off, per-site setting from a run-in, data needs, or evaluation against the justified drop; its prediction heads aim at policy-invariant states, i.e. towards ignoring the pattern. Reliability concerns: no named datasets ("a multi-site EHR corpus"), very broad claims with tight intervals, padded and partly irrelevant references (e.g. a dialogue-policy survey cited for the observation policy, cubic splines for quantum operations, stock-market ARIMA), a broken citation. Cite as related work on separating physiology from policy; do not rely on its numbers.

## The dial above 1 (19 Oct 2026, exact models, sigma 1, A gamma 2, n 6,000)

Choosing w on B's data can include w > 1. Brier at B:

| B's testing | B optimum | w = 0 | w = 0.5 | w = 1 | w = 1.25 | w = 1.5 | w = 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| less selective (gamma 1) | 0.1071 | 0.1126 | **0.1079** | 0.1092 | 0.1109 | 0.1131 | 0.1190 |
| more selective (gamma 3.5) | 0.1008 | 0.1186 | 0.1038 | **0.1015** | 0.1016 | 0.1023 | 0.1049 |

Turning the dial up barely helps when B is more selective: scaling the testing log-likelihood is not the same as a steeper testing curve, and the A model loses little there (0.0007 above B's optimum, against 0.0021 when B is less selective). Over-trusting the pattern costs more than under-trusting it, so with uncertainty about B a dial at or below 1 is the safer default; re-learning the testing part at B handles both directions.

## Silent run-ins and the data the dial needs (19 Oct 2026; `out/lit/lit7_silent_trials.md`, E6)

Documented silent (shadow) runs of sepsis models: Duke Sepsis Watch about 3 months; UCSD COMPOSER about 46 days (754 ED encounters); Penn early-warning about 3 months (10,448 discharges); a paediatric ward 1 year (68 cases); Epic ESMv2 at Tufts (preprint) about 77 days, about 32,000 hospitalisations and 1,049 Sepsis-3 cases (about 95 cases a week). TREWS, Michigan and Kaiser report none. Guidance (Kwong et al. 2022, TRIPOD+AI, FDA change-control plans) fixes no duration. Local fine-tuning studies used about 1,500 target patients (Holder et al.) or 10% of a site's data (Moor et al.). No study measures the data needed to set a single trust parameter (C5 stays open).

E6 (STATUS.md): on Beth Israel to Emory, choosing w is stable from about 1,000 patients (about 22 cases); a full refit of the linear model overtakes from about 2,500 patients (53 cases). For E6's ICU cohort that is far less than a large hospital's typical run-in, so the dial's case rests on small sites, short run-ins and large models, and on the fact that the best end differs per target (Futoma: drop for Memphis; E6: keep for Emory).

### Cases per run-in (19 Oct 2026; `out/lit/lit8_runin_case_counts.md`, references there)

- ICU volume: median US study ICU 16 beds and 1,299 admissions a year (Checkley et al. 2014, 69 centres); England 580-675 admissions per unit a year (Wilcox et al. 2014; ICNARC 2022-23); Netherlands about 513 per ICU (2011). About 85% of ICU stays last at least 24 h (one Canadian cohort).
- Derived with E6's outcome rate (2% of eligible patients develop sepsis in the next 48 h): one median US ICU yields about 2-6 cases in a 1-3 month run-in, an English unit 1-3; reaching E6's refit crossover (about 50 cases) would take about 2.3 years in one US ICU. A hospital with about 82 ICU beds yields about 9-28 cases in 1-3 months, a three-hospital system 28-84. Hospital-wide deployments are far larger (Tufts ESMv2: 1,049 cases in about 2.5 months over three hospitals).
- Updating literature: no universal event minimum; external validation alone needs at least 100 events (200 for calibration curves; Riley et al. 2024), i.e. about 5,000 eligible patients at 2%. Steyerberg et al. 2004 (samples from 14 to 700 events) and Vergouwe et al. 2017 found parsimonious updates (intercept, recalibration) preferable at small samples and full coefficient revision prone to overfitting. Moor et al. 2023 fine-tuned a deep sepsis model on 10% of each target site's data; event counts not reported.
- Reading: a single ICU's silent run-in sits well below E6's crossover, where one-parameter updates win; this is the setting for the dial. Large hospital-wide deployments can afford a refit of a simple model. Corrections to lit7: Duke's and UCSD's silent-run denominators and case counts are not reported in the papers checked here.
